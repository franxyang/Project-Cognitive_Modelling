# Phase 1 Workbook

See also [[Layered Learning Hub]].

## Goal

Phase 1 is not about reading many papers.
It is about building a stable concept map in plain language before deeper paper-by-paper reading.

You should not move on until you can explain the core concepts without hiding behind jargon.

## How to use this workbook

For each concept block:

1. read the short explanation
2. compare it with the linked concept note
3. answer the self-check questions aloud
4. rewrite the idea in your own words

## Unit 1: CLT core

### Cognitive load

Plain meaning:
`Cognitive load` is the amount of mental burden placed on working memory during learning or problem solving.

Why it matters:
This is the broad umbrella concept.
Your model needs to unpack it into more precise components instead of treating it as one vague number.

Easy confusion:
It is not the same as difficulty in general.
It is specifically about the burden placed on limited working-memory processing.

Project consequence:
You are not modeling "hardness" in the abstract.
You are modeling how properties of information and presentation consume limited processing capacity.

See:
- [[intrinsic-vs-extraneous-load]]
- [[working-memory-resource]]

### Intrinsic load

Plain meaning:
`Intrinsic load` comes from the material itself, especially from how many parts have to be understood together.

Why it matters:
This is the closest conceptual ancestor of your intrinsic cost term.

Easy confusion:
It is not just the number of items.
It depends on relational coordination and the learner's prior knowledge.

Project consequence:
Your model should not define intrinsic cost only by amount.
It also needs element complexity, interaction, and learner-relative novelty.

See:
- [[intrinsic-vs-extraneous-load]]
- [[element-interactivity]]
- [[schema-and-prior-knowledge]]

### Extraneous load

Plain meaning:
`Extraneous load` comes from the way material is presented, organized, or formatted rather than from the structure of the content itself.

Why it matters:
This is the main justification for a separate `E_i` term.

Easy confusion:
It is not everything that feels effortful.
Some effort comes from genuine content structure and belongs to intrinsic load instead.

Project consequence:
You need a way to distinguish cost caused by information relations from cost caused by poor segmentation, interface, or representation.

See:
- [[intrinsic-vs-extraneous-load]]
- [[segmentation-vs-chunking]]

### Germane processing

Plain meaning:
`Germane processing` refers to mental effort directed toward useful learning, such as building or refining schemas.

Why it matters:
In older CLT language it was sometimes treated like another load category.
In later accounts it is better understood as useful allocation of available resources.

Easy confusion:
It is not simply a third independent source of burden.

Project consequence:
Your `P_i` term should be interpreted as the remaining goal-relevant processing available after intrinsic and extraneous costs are paid.

See:
- [[intrinsic-vs-extraneous-load]]
- [[working-memory-resource]]

### Element interactivity

Plain meaning:
`Element interactivity` is about how many pieces of information must be coordinated at the same time for understanding.

Why it matters:
This is the most important CLT concept for your model.

Easy confusion:
It is not the same as the number of elements.
Ten independent facts can be easier than three tightly linked relations.

Project consequence:
This is what justifies relation terms like `w_uv` rather than a pure count model.

See:
- [[element-interactivity]]
- [[task-complexity-vs-task-difficulty]]

### Unit 1 synthesis

CLT gives you a powerful starting vocabulary:
- working memory is limited
- some burden comes from content structure
- some burden comes from presentation
- useful learning depends on what capacity remains for goal-relevant processing

What CLT does not yet give you is a precise mathematical representation of these ideas.

## Unit 2: Working memory core

### Working memory

Plain meaning:
`Working memory` is the system used to keep information temporarily available and manipulate it during reasoning, comprehension, and learning.

Why it matters:
Your whole model is built around the idea that this resource is limited.

Easy confusion:
It is not just short-term storage.
It also includes active processing.

See:
- [[working-memory-resource]]

### Storage versus processing

Plain meaning:
Working memory is not only about holding items.
It is also about coordinating, comparing, updating, and transforming them.

Why it matters:
Your project is not just about how many items fit.
It is about the processing burden of structured information.

### Slot versus resource

Plain meaning:
A slot view says memory has a small number of discrete item places.
A resource view says there is a limited but flexible processing budget that can be distributed across items.

Why it matters:
The resource view is more useful for your model because it supports graded allocation and optimization.

Easy confusion:
A resource view does not mean capacity is unlimited.
It means quality can vary depending on allocation.

See:
- [[working-memory-resource]]
- [[resource-rationality]]

### Allocable resource and precision

Plain meaning:
Memory capacity can be treated as something that can be invested unevenly.
Some elements or relations may receive more precise representation than others.

Why it matters:
This makes your `C_i` term more than a verbal capacity ceiling.

Project consequence:
Later versions of the model may need to decide whether capacity is fixed, strategic, or costly to deploy.

## Unit 3: Coherence and learning

### Textbase versus situation model

Plain meaning:
The `textbase` is a representation of what the text literally says.
The `situation model` is a deeper representation of the underlying state of affairs, relations, and meaning.

Why it matters:
A learner can remember statements without building a usable relational model.

See:
- [[recall-vs-transfer]]
- [[coherence-and-connection-loss]]

### Coherence

Plain meaning:
`Coherence` means the information is connected in a way that supports understanding across elements rather than only within isolated pieces.

Why it matters:
This is the theoretical basis for caring about cross-segment relations.

Project consequence:
If segmentation damages coherence, local relief may come at the cost of later transfer.

### Recall versus transfer

Plain meaning:
`Recall` is reproducing learned content.
`Transfer` is using learned relations or principles in a new setting.

Why it matters:
Your model should not predict a single generic outcome.

Project consequence:
Transfer should usually be more sensitive than recall to fragmentation and lost relations.

## Unit 4: Formal-model core

### Segmentation versus partitioning

Plain meaning:
`Segmentation` is an instructional strategy that splits material into parts.
`Partitioning` is the more abstract modeling language for dividing structured information into segments.

Why it matters:
Your model is written in partition language, but it is motivated by segmentation problems.

See:
- [[segmentation-vs-chunking]]

### Chunking and compression

Plain meaning:
`Chunking` groups several elements into a more manageable unit.
`Compression` keeps some structure while reducing representational burden.

Why it matters:
These are neighboring ideas, but not the same as your problem.

Project consequence:
They help justify why information organization under limits can be studied formally.

### Resource-rationality

Plain meaning:
Cognition can be modeled as making trade-offs under costs and limits rather than as failing to be perfectly rational.

Why it matters:
This is the conceptual bridge from theory talk to constrained optimization.

See:
- [[resource-rationality]]

### Rate-distortion

Plain meaning:
There is a principled trade-off between how accurately information is represented and how much resource is spent.

Why it matters:
This supports the idea that preserving more structure may require more capacity.

## Phase 1 completion test

You are ready to move on only if you can answer these aloud:

1. What is cognitive load?
2. What is the difference between intrinsic and extraneous load?
3. Why is element interactivity not the same as amount?
4. Why should prior knowledge affect effective cost?
5. Why is working memory not just storage?
6. Why is a resource view more useful than a fixed-slot view for this project?
7. Why can segmentation help and hurt at the same time?
8. Why should transfer be more sensitive than recall to fragmentation?
9. Why is this project not just chunking?
10. Why is optimization language justified here at all?
