# Theory-Only Literature Strategy

## Short answer

Your current Zotero collection is not wrong.
It is just misweighted for a theory-first project.

Right now, the set is strong on:

- classic cognitive load theory,
- working-memory foundations,
- measurement,
- multimedia-learning applications.

But if you are not planning to design and run an experiment, then the collection is relatively weak on:

- formal models of comprehension and coherence,
- mathematical or computational models of working-memory allocation,
- rational chunking or compression accounts,
- and neighboring theory papers that can help position your model against existing frameworks.

## What should stay central

These papers still belong in the core:

- Sweller (1988)
- Sweller (2010)

## Missing literature lanes

### 1. Comprehension and coherence models

Your model is not only about memory limits.
It is also about preserving structured relations across information elements.

That means you should add literature on how readers or learners build coherent mental representations from connected propositions.
The most relevant starting point is Kintsch.

Recommended addition:

- Kintsch, W. (1994). *Text comprehension, memory, and learning.* American Psychologist. DOI: `10.1037/0003-066X.49.4.294`

Why it matters:

- it gives you a serious theory of coherence, prior knowledge, and learning from text,
- it helps justify why segmentation can preserve or destroy a meaningful structure,
- and it gives you a neighboring model of comprehension that is much closer to your `L(Pi)` idea than standard cognitive load questionnaires are.

### 2. Rational chunking and compression

Your current model already leans toward partitioning and information compression.
You should therefore read work that treats chunking as an adaptive response to capacity limits.

Recommended addition:

- Nassar, Helmers, and Frank (2018). *Chunking as a rational strategy for lossy data compression in visual working memory.* Psychological Review. DOI: `10.1037/rev0000101`

Why it matters:

- it gives you a direct theoretical neighbor for partitioning under limits,
- it helps you think about when combining elements is beneficial,
- and it may sharpen how you justify the trade-off between tractability and fidelity.

### 3. Information-theoretic or normative memory models

You already have one strong optimization paper in van den Berg and Ma (2018).
That is good, but it should not stand alone.

Recommended additions:

- Jakob and Gershman (2023). *Rate-distortion theory of neural coding and its implications for working memory.* eLife. DOI: `10.7554/eLife.79450`
- Yoo, Klyszejko, Curtis, and Ma (2018). *Strategic allocation of working memory resource.* Scientific Reports. DOI: `10.1038/s41598-018-34282-1`

Why they matter:

- Jakob and Gershman provide a principled rate-distortion framework for memory limits,
- Yoo et al. show strategic allocation of working-memory resources by behavioral priority,
- together they make your optimization framing look less isolated and more like part of a serious modeling tradition.

### 4. Neighboring non-mathematical design models

You should know the major neighboring frameworks even if you do not build on them directly.

The most important are:

- cognitive theory of multimedia learning (CTML),
- four-component instructional design (4C/ID).

Why:

- CTML is a major adjacent theory of how words and graphics are processed,
- 4C/ID is probably the most comprehensive instructional-design framework linked to cognitive load theory,
- neither is the same as your model,
- but you need to know where your model sits relative to them.

## Existing models already near your topic

These are the main ones already in range of your project:

### Cognitive load theory

This is the verbal-theoretical parent framework.
It gives you the problem space, but not yet the mathematics you want.

### Sawicka's system-dynamics model

This is the closest direct precedent for a formal treatment of CLT.
It shows that formalization is already legitimate inside the field.
It does not yet give you graph structure or optimization.

### Resource-rational working-memory models

van den Berg and Ma (2018), and related resource-allocation work, are your best precedents for:

- explicit objective functions,
- variable capacity investment,
- performance-cost trade-offs.

### Construction-integration style comprehension models

Kintsch-style models are highly relevant because they treat comprehension as building a connected semantic representation under constraints and prior knowledge.
That is conceptually close to your idea of structural preservation and connection loss.

### Rational chunking and compression models

Nassar-style work is relevant because your proposed partitioning problem is partly a compression problem.
These papers may help you sharpen the conditions under which segmentation or chunking is adaptive.

## Do you need neuroscience or computational neuroscience?

### Plain neuroscience or EEG workload papers

Usually no, not for the core theory paper.

They are helpful only if you want one of these:

- an observation layer for latent processing states,
- a biological plausibility section,
- or a future empirical validation path.

They should not dominate your current review.

### Computational neuroscience of working memory

Yes, selectively.

This is the part of neuroscience that is actually useful for you.
It helps when it does one of these:

- models allocation of limited resources,
- formalizes memory distortion or precision trade-offs,
- explains chunking, compression, or prioritization,
- or links cognitive limits to neural coding costs.

That is why papers like:

- van den Berg and Ma (2018),
- Jakob and Gershman (2023),
- Yoo et al. (2018),

are much more valuable for you than generic EEG classification papers.

## Bottom line

If you remove the experiment from the project, your current set is not unusable.
But it is too education-measurement heavy and not theory-model heavy enough.

The most important rebalance is:

1. less emphasis on load measurement,
2. less emphasis on generic multimedia effects,
3. more emphasis on comprehension structure,
4. more emphasis on rational allocation and compression,
5. selective use of computational neuroscience,
6. minimal use of generic neuroscience.
